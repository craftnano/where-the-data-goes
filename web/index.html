    About the data & methodology

    A map of where personal data flows between organizations, one edge per publicly disclosed sub-processor relationship. Collected October 2026.

    What this is

    Where the Data Goes is a visualization of publicly disclosed sub-processor relationships. Sub-processors are a specific legal category: third parties that process personal data (usually customer or consumer data) on behalf of the companies shown, as defined under GDPR Article 28. Each edge in the map is a company stating, under contract, that a given third party receives personal data from it.

    The piece is a map of personal-data egress between organizations, not of software architecture. Anything a company self-hosts (Postgres, Kafka, DuckDB, Redis, Elasticsearch, Grafana, Nginx, open-source infrastructure in general) is invisible here because no personal data crosses a corporate boundary. Think of each edge as an ingress/egress declaration, not a dependency.

    What this is not

    Not a map of all vendor relationships. Every company shown has many additional third parties that do not appear here: SaaS tools handling non-personal data (procurement, finance, internal analytics), APIs that do not involve personally identifiable information, consultants, agencies, infrastructure vendors with no personal-data access, data brokers that purchase rather than process, and so on. None of those relationships trigger a public sub-processor disclosure, so none are mapped.

    Not a map of breach surface. In the event of a security incident, the exposure surface is not limited to the edges shown here. The companies in this map typically have dozens or hundreds of additional vendor and API relationships beyond their sub-processor list, almost all of them undisclosed publicly. Treating this map as a complete inventory of a company's exposure would be a mistake.

    Not an infrastructure map. Self-hosted databases, caches, queues, observability stacks, and development tools are structurally absent. The real architecture of any company shown here is the edges you see plus whatever runs inside its own cloud account.

    Data sources

    This visualization is based on information collected from publicly accessible sources during October 2026.

    Public sub-processor disclosures have become increasingly common in the GDPR era. Disclosure practices vary across organizations, services, and customer types. Some companies publish information covering both business-to-business and business-to-consumer relationships; others disclose only through private contractual arrangements.

    This project uses the best publicly available information identified through its research. No materials requiring acceptance of a nondisclosure agreement or other confidentiality obligation were accessed or incorporated.

    AI-assisted research & accuracy

    Artificial intelligence tools identified, extracted, and structured sub-processor entries from published source materials. Each observation preserves the source URL and verbatim purpose text where possible. The pipeline is open-sourced at github.com/craftnano/where-the-data-goes, so corrections are reproducible.

    AI-assisted research can introduce errors, omissions, or misinterpretations. The visualization is provided on a best-effort basis. Corrections and feedback are welcome. Open an issue or pull request on the repository.

    Scope & limitations

    Not an inventory. The visualization is not an exhaustive map of corporate sub-processor relationships. The absence of a connection does not establish that no relationship exists.

    Disclosed relationships only. Connections represent relationships identified in published disclosures, not independently verified data transfers. They do not establish the nature, volume, sensitivity, or frequency of any data processed.

    Point-in-time. Source information was collected during October 2026 unless otherwise noted on an edge (archive-sourced edges carry the Wayback capture date). Relationships and disclosures may have changed since.

    No assessment of security posture. Inclusion in the visualization does not imply a vulnerability, security deficiency, regulatory violation, or inappropriate data-sharing practice.

    B2B over B2C skew. The dataset over-represents B2B SaaS companies, which disclose named vendors under Article 28. Consumer-facing businesses (healthcare, retail, mobility, finance) typically disclose categories of recipients under Articles 13/14 rather than named sub-processors, so they appear here as "categories only" rather than as mapped flows. This is a feature of the disclosure regime, not a crawl failure.
